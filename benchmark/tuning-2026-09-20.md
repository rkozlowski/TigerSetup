# Internals tuning session, 2026-09-20: measurements

Focused measurements taken on the build machine (AMD Zen 3, 16 logical
CPUs, NVMe, Windows 11 26200, Windows Defender real-time protection on)
against the committed **TigerSetup 0.9.0** baseline, to decide five
internals questions. Each section states what was measured, the numbers,
and what was kept. Nothing here is a lab number, and nothing here rewrites
`report.md`, `report-0.9.0.md` or the recorded 0.7.1/0.8.0/0.9.0 results.

The measurement scripts are the repository's own (`scripts/
Measure-LocalUninstall.ps1`, `scripts/ProcessTreeMeter.psm1`, the
compression spike's `cspike run`) plus three disposable session scripts
whose method is described inline. The corpus run's machine-readable
result is `compression-spike/results/stage2b-order.json`.

## 1. SQLite: the commit group replaces the batch as the durability unit

**Question.** 0.9.0 made the builder's file batch the journal's unit and
paid two durable commits per batch, and one per registry value, PATH
entry, environment variable and firewall rule. Does the recovery model
need every batch, and every such resource, to be a durable commit of its
own?

**Answer.** No. What the invariant constrains is that the undo record is
durable before the mutation and the mutation before the record of it; it
does not say how many operations share a commit. The forward walk now
takes up to **eight consecutive journal batches** as one *commit group*:
every undo record of the group in one commit, the mutations in sequence
order, every `applied` record in one more. A crash inside a group leaves
the whole group `applying`, and recovery reconciles it by inspection
exactly as it reconciled a batch; the group bound (≤ 2,048 files or 256
MiB, plus files larger than a batch) is the bound on that work. Registry
values, PATH entries, environment variables, firewall rules and stored
action programs now share batches with their neighbours of the same kind
— their undo is captured before any mutation of the group, which is the
same guarantee "committed immediately before its own mutation" gave — so
only a custom action, and an operation an injected fault names, is a
group of its own. A fresh database is also created at the current schema
in one commit rather than eight. `TigerSetup-Design.md` §5.4 is the
owning text.

**Measurement.** `scripts/Measure-LocalUninstall.ps1`, five repetitions,
median engine span; the commit count is the `journal_commits=` of the
`transaction_committed` event. The 0.9.0 column is the committed
baseline's binaries; the "commit group" column is the same build machine
with the change, immediately afterwards.

| Shape | Commits, install: 0.9.0 → group | Commits, uninstall | Install engine span | Uninstall engine span |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,200 × 450 KB (553 MB) | 61 → **9** | 52 → **8** | 3.45 s → **3.30 s** | 1.51 s → **1.29 s** |
| 38 × 6 MB (240 MB) | 43 → **7** | 34 → **6** | 0.54 s → **0.46 s** | 0.15 s → **0.08 s** |
| 60 × 120 KB (7 MB) | 29 → **5** | 20 → **4** | 0.20 s → **0.15 s** | 0.12 s → **0.08 s** |

The install's commits are now: the schema (1, fresh database), `begin`
(1), two per commit group, and the ownership commit (1); the 1,200-file
install has three groups (the directories' batch and seven file batches;
eight file batches; the last two file batches with the registration's
key and values). The `forward_walk_profile` event the walk now logs puts
the journal at 28 ms of the 1,200-file install's 3.3 s walk and 5 ms of
the small application's 120 ms — about 5 ms per commit, the large
`applying` commit included — so the journal has stopped being a term
worth measuring on this drive; on a slower one the count is what scales.

**Recovery reasoning.** The invariant is unchanged and is checked at the
same boundaries: no mutation of a group begins before every undo record
of the group is durable; no `applied` record is written before every
mutation it describes is durable (files are flushed and renamed
write-through as they are written, hives are flushed before the
transaction commits, exactly as before). What changed is only how much a
restart may have to inspect — a group instead of a batch — and the bound
on that is stated, tested and eight times the old one. The local
crash-recovery suite (`crates/tigersetup-setup/tests/durability.rs`) was
extended with a ten-batch package that crashes inside the second and the
eighth file batch and asserts the journal states of every file and what
recovery rewrites; the fault boundaries' semantics — everything before
the named operation durably applied, nothing after it started — hold by
construction, because such an operation is a group of its own. The lab's
recovery rows, unchanged, are the acceptance: the 0.10.0 release validation
(2026-09-21) ran all eleven on the release bytes — ten PASS and the reboot
row's usual WARN (the interrupted run's log lost its unflushed tail after
the reboot; every recovery check of the row passed).

**Files changed:** `crates/tigersetup-engine/src/txn/executor.rs` (the
group walk and the profile), `state/journal.rs` (group transitions),
`state/db.rs` (one-commit schema creation, commit timing), `plan.rs`
(every typed resource batched), `txn/rollback.rs` and `txn/mod.rs`
(comments, the exported bound); `crates/tigersetup-setup/tests/
durability.rs` and `tests/common/mod.rs`; `TigerSetup-Design.md` §5.4.

## 2. Zstandard multithreaded build compression

**Question.** The fresh benchmark measured TigerSetup's release build at
CPU/wall ≈ 1 and about twice Inno Setup's wall time. Does Zstandard's
multithreaded compression (`ZSTD_c_nbWorkers`, the `zstdmt` feature of
the `zstd` crate) give the builder its cores back without costing the
installer anything?

**Method.** The `zstdmt` feature was enabled and the payload encoder given
the worker count from an environment variable, for this measurement
only; the four benchmark packages were built release-quality under the
process-tree meter (`scripts/ProcessTreeMeter.psm1`: wall, CPU, peak job
commit charge) with 0 (the product's single-threaded encoder), 2, 4 and
8 workers, and the installers' bytes compared. The engine block is
unaffected by the feature (2,487,808 B either way: the engine links the
decoder alone).

**Determinism.** For a given worker count ≥ 1 the output is identical for
2, 4 and 8 workers — the job layout is a function of the parameters, not
of the thread count — and it is stable across runs; it differs from the
single-threaded output. So a multithreaded builder could choose its
worker count per machine and still ship one set of bytes per package.

**The trade.** With long-distance matching on, libzstd sizes a job from
the chain log rather than the window (`ZSTDMT_computeTargetJobLog`), and
a job's shared history with the previous one is the overlap, at most a
quarter of the job; the 128 MiB window does not reach across jobs. That
is where the wall time comes from — 1.3× on WinMerge (one job), 2.2× on
VLC, 2.5× on qBittorrent, 3.9× on ShareX at eight workers — and where the
bytes go: +0.01 % to +0.88 % (VLC, whose duplicated plug-in DLLs the
window was finding across the whole payload). CPU rises 5–38 %, and the
builder's peak commit charge goes from 228–279 MB to 0.7–2.2 GB.

**Result: no change; the default stays single-threaded.** The release
build is paid once per version and the bytes are downloaded and decoded
on every installation; +0.9 % on a payload is the size of gain the
ordering work below was chasing, and 2 GB of builder memory is the
gratuitous behaviour the question excluded. The finding is recorded in
`TigerSetup-Design.md` §10.4 so the question is not reopened without new
evidence; the experiment hook and the feature were removed. If the
Architect ever weighs build time above installer bytes, four workers is
the setting the numbers support (eight buys ShareX 13 s more for 0.9 GB
more memory, and nothing for the others).

order. Would a small static type family (text / module / image /
compressed / font / other), or putting same-named files side by side
across directories, compress better?

(`tool/src/layout.rs`: `ext-basename-path`, `type-path`,

15-payload corpus — the four benchmark applications and the eleven spike

admitted to the stream (`cspike run`, 90 jobs, six at a time; sizes are

**Result: no change.** The 128 MiB window already reaches the duplicated
binaries and resources that basename locality was meant to bring
together, so putting same-named files side by side gains nothing and,
by breaking directory locality within an extension, loses a little
(`ext-basename-path` +0.10%). The type family alone is neutral (+0.03%);
with the extension kept inside it, it is −0.06% over the corpus but not
uniformly — VS Code −0.73% and Git for Windows −1.06% against Inkscape
+1.22% — which is a per-application coin toss, not the deterministic ~1%
that would justify a second ordering rule. `type-basename-ext-path`, the
strongest form of the hypothesis, is the worst (+0.72%, Git for Windows
+4.98%). `ext-path` stays.

## 4. First launch: the delay before the engine runs

**Question.** The 0.9.0 clean-VM benchmark saw 1.96–5.47 s between the
installer's start and the engine's `run_started`, growing with the whole
installer's size although the loader reads only the 1.1 MB engine block,
and a later run of the same installer (the uninstall) did not pay it. Is
that Windows' first-execution handling of a file it has not seen?

**Method** (this machine, Defender real-time protection on, engine
1.1.26080.3; times are whole-process wall clocks of `Setup.exe inspect
--json`, which reads the footer and the metadata and installs nothing;
the four release-quality benchmark installers built above):

| Installer | MB | copy A: 1st, 2nd, 3rd run | copy B (same bytes, new name): 1st, 2nd | `MpCmdRun -Scan` of copy C | copy C: 1st run after that scan |
|---|---:|---|---|---:|---:|
| WinMerge | 18.1 | 212, 165, 164 ms | 209, 166 ms | 62 ms | 217 ms |
| VLC | 40.4 | 222, 167, 174 ms | 227, 166 ms | 39 ms | 235 ms |
| qBittorrent | 45.8 | 214, 166, 169 ms | 237, 167 ms | 39 ms | 230 ms |
| ShareX | 144.5 | 250, 166, 166 ms | 268, 177 ms | 42 ms | 252 ms |

And the fixed part, split: the engine started directly (`tigersetup-
setup.exe inspect`, no loader) takes 14 ms; through the loader, 165 ms;
a fresh copy of the engine executable started directly takes 250 ms the
first time and 14 ms the second, four times out of four.

**What the evidence says.**

- A file's first execution costs extra and its second does not, and the
  extra returns for a byte-identical copy under another name: it is
  per-file-identity handling by the platform, not anything in the bytes.
  It grows with the installer's size (+45 ms at 18 MB, +85 ms at 145 MB
  on top of the fixed cost), and an explicit scanner pass beforehand
  does not remove it, so it is the real-time engine's own first-open
  work rather than a cached verdict.
- The lab's 2–5.5 s were not reproduced here: this machine had seen
  today's loader and engine bytes in many installers before the probe,
  where the clean VM sees a never-executed binary of unknown reputation
  and, with cloud-delivered protection, may hold it for a cloud lookup.
  The direction and the shape match the benchmark's observation; the
  magnitude is the VM's, and only the VM can confirm the mechanism.
- **Every run pays ~150 ms for the engine the loader extracts.** The
  loader writes the engine to a fresh temporary each time — by design:
  a name that already exists is never adopted, so nothing pre-placed
  can be executed — and Windows treats that fresh 2.5 MB executable as
  never seen. That is most of the 165 ms an otherwise 14 ms command
  takes through the loader, install and uninstall alike.

**Product change: none**, as the question asked. Two things are worth
knowing tomorrow: on the VM, a second `inspect` of the same installer
and a first `inspect` of a renamed copy would tell whether the VM's
seconds are per-file-identity handling like this machine's milliseconds
(cheap, two commands, no product change); and the loader's fresh
temporary is a deliberate security choice whose price is now known
(~150 ms per run here) — reusing a verified, hash-named extraction under
the user's own directory would remove it, and is an Architect question
about the loader's contract, not a tuning.

2,489,344 B raw, 1,146,190 B as the compressed block):

Plus 320 KB `.rdata`, 58 KB `.pdata`, 46 KB `.xdata`, 19 KB `.rsrc`.

Nothing was cut. The one cheap experiment — clap without `color` and
`suggestions` — measured 1,146,190 → 1,135,598 B compressed (−0.9%) and
was reverted: the 2026-09-11 size audit had already judged those ~10 KB
not worth the plainer diagnostics, and the number did not move. The
catalog stack is a designed capability (the hint refresh at install
time), and SQLite's remaining `OMIT` options are the ones the amalgamation
does not support. The C loader (74,752 B) was not looked at.

**Runtime stages.** The forward walk now logs one
`forward_walk_profile` event — the walk's wall time, the time in each
stage accumulated over every operation, the journal's time and commit
count, and the files and bytes written; one `Instant::now()` pair per
stage per file, nothing per byte. The `--fast` synthetic packages of the
journal measurement, medians of five, on this NVMe drive:

| Shape | walk | prepare | stage (decode + SHA-256 + write) | flush | rename (write-through) | other | journal |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1,200 × 450 KB install | 3,247 ms | 26 | 980 | 1,054 | 1,038 | 5 | 28 |
| 1,200 × 450 KB uninstall | 1,004 ms | 52 | — | — | — | 909 (file removals) | 32 |
| 38 × 6 MB install | 424 ms | 1 | 261 | 103 | 33 | 1 | 9 |
| 60 × 120 KB install | 120 ms | 1 | 27 | 29 | 48 | 2 | 5 |

Two findings, neither changed today:

- **The per-file cost is the two durability points, not the bytes.**
  Decoding, hashing and writing 553 MB takes 0.98 s (≈ 560 MB/s through
  a level-3 stream, SHA-NI and the cache); making each file durable —
  `FlushFileBuffers` on the temporary, then a `MOVEFILE_WRITE_THROUGH`
  rename — takes 2.1 s, about 1.7 ms per file, and an uninstall's
  write-through rename of each file into the staging area is the same
  0.75 ms per file. This is the file-count term the benchmark saw
  (WinMerge's 472 files against qBittorrent's 38). The commit group
  makes a principled change possible — rename without write-through and
  make the group's renames durable together before its `applied` commit,
  as the hives already are — but that rests on NTFS log semantics
  (a flush forcing the log to the latest LSN, which is monotone across
  the volume) that a local test cannot establish and a power-cut row
  can only fail to refute. It is the one candidate with a measurable
  prize (≈ 1 s of the 3.3 s here; more on a slower disk) and it is a
  durability decision, so it is reported, not taken.
- **A run's fixed cost is outside the walk**: the engine's own start is
  14 ms; the loader's extraction adds ~150 ms of the platform's
  first-execution handling (§4), and writing the state directory's
  uninstaller copy another ~30 ms. Nothing in the walk is worth more
  than the rename question above.

No runtime change was retained beyond the profile event itself and the
journal's commit groups (§1).
